<p>Three letters can decide whether you study abroad, land a job overseas, or get turned away at the door: IELTS, TOEFL, or one of the Cambridge exams. Most candidates pour months into vocabulary lists and grammar drills, yet walk into the exam hall not knowing how the test itself is built. That is a costly mistake. Each of these exams rewards a different kind of preparation, and the shape of the test — its timing, its task types, its scoring criteria — determines how you should train for it.</p>
<h2>IELTS: built for speed and skimming</h2>
<p>IELTS splits into four sections, each testing a distinct skill under its own clock. Reading gives you 60 minutes for 40 questions, and the real challenge is rarely vocabulary — it is speed. The test rewards skimming and scanning over careful, line-by-line reading, and it wants you to spot a writer&#39;s opinion, not just a fact. Writing follows the same 60-minute window across two tasks: a Task 1 report or letter, and a Task 2 essay, each judged on its own weighting. Listening runs 30 minutes across four sections, and its traps are subtler than they look — spelling mistakes and deliberate distractors cost more marks than genuine mishearing. Speaking closes the exam with an 11-to-14-minute conversation in three parts, scored above all on fluency, coherence, and how varied your vocabulary is under pressure.</p>
<h2>TOEFL iBT: academic stamina</h2>
<p>TOEFL iBT is built for a different endurance test: sustained academic listening and synthesis. Reading gives you 35 minutes across two passages, focused on main ideas, supporting details, and inference rather than raw speed. Listening is the section that catches people off guard — 36 minutes covering three lectures and two conversations, demanding pragmatic understanding and the ability to synthesize information across a long stretch of speech, not just recall isolated facts. Speaking takes 16 minutes across four tasks that blend independent opinion with integrated skills, meaning you often have to read, listen, and then speak in response to both. Writing closes things out in 29 minutes across two tasks, one of them an integrated task that pairs reading and listening input with an academic discussion response.</p>
<h2>Cambridge exams: precision over speed</h2>
<p>FCE, CAE and CPE — the Cambridge suite — trade the pure-speed pressure of IELTS for something closer to linguistic precision. The Reading and Use of English paper folds in word formation, key-word transformations, and open cloze tasks that test grammatical control at a granular level. Writing requires two tasks: a mandatory essay plus a choice from formats like letters, reports, or reviews, so flexibility across registers matters. Listening spans four parts mixing multiple choice, sentence completion, and multiple matching, while Speaking runs four parts including an individual long turn and a collaborative task with another candidate — a format that rewards not just accuracy but the ability to negotiate a conversation in real time.</p>

<Callout type="tip" title="Scoring criteria are not decoration">
<p>Every one of these exams grades against a published rubric — Task Achievement, Coherence and Cohesion, Lexical Resource, and Grammatical Range and Accuracy are the recurring pillars. Practising without checking your answers against these categories is practising blind.</p>

<h2>Strategy follows structure</h2>
<p>Once the shape of the exam is clear, the right strategy falls out of it almost automatically. Active reading — hunting for markers like <em>however</em> or <em>consequently</em> — pays off wherever inference is tested, which is everywhere. Paraphrasing, swapping in synonyms and restructuring sentences, is rewarded explicitly in Cambridge&#39;s Use of English paper and implicitly in every Writing and Speaking section across all three exams. Time management deserves special care: because each task carries its own point value, spending eight minutes perfecting a two-point answer while a ten-point task goes untouched is a losing trade, regardless of which exam you sit.</p>

<h2>Choosing your exam, not just your score</h2>
<p>The temptation is to treat these exams as interchangeable hurdles, each demanding the same generic &quot;good English.&quot; They do not. IELTS rewards the fast skimmer, TOEFL rewards the academic synthesizer, and Cambridge rewards the grammatical precisionist. Knowing this before you open a single practice book lets you direct your energy where the exam will actually spend it — and that, more than any vocabulary list, is what separates a target score from a disappointing one.</p>
